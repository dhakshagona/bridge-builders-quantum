High school essentials: credits, GPA, and graduation

Credits and graduation

Students earn credits by passing classes. Schools require a set number across English, math, science, social studies, PE/health, arts, and electives; the exact list varies by district and state.

  • Ask for a graduation-credit audit and school handbook in the family’s preferred language.
  • Review the transcript each semester for completed credits, failed or missing courses, and requirements still needed.
  • Ask whether state tests, civic requirements, service hours, attendance, or a financial-aid form are needed to graduate.
  • For students transferring from abroad or another school, ask how prior coursework will be evaluated.

GPA and class rank

GPA summarizes grades. Schools may use a 4.0 scale, a weighted scale that adds value for advanced classes, or both. Some also show class rank.

  • Ask whether the transcript includes weighted and unweighted GPA.
  • Colleges look at grades, course rigor, and improvement—not just one number.
  • After a low grade, ask early about tutoring, teacher office hours, make-up work, credit recovery, and a study plan.
Course choices: Honors, AP, IB, dual credit, and summer classes

Build a four-year course plan

  • Meet with the counselor before registration every year; compare plans with graduation and college-entry requirements.
  • College-prep patterns commonly include four years of English; three to four years each of math, lab science, social studies, and world language; plus arts and electives. Each college has its own rules.
  • Continue math through senior year when possible. Select challenging courses that are sustainable with available support.
  • English learner supports, special education, and accommodations should be part of planning—not barriers to long-term goals.

Advanced and college-level options

  • Honors: more rigorous high-school classes; GPA policy varies.
  • AP: college-level courses with optional exams. Scores may earn college credit or placement depending on the college.
  • IB: an internationally recognized program with courses, assessments, and a possible diploma.
  • Dual credit / dual enrollment: college courses taken in high school. Confirm transfer acceptance, fees, workload, and whether the course creates a college transcript.
  • Summer credit / CBE: summer classes, online credit recovery, or credit by examination may help; get school approval first.
Parent portals, interpreters, and the school support team

Stay connected

  • Set up the parent/family portal for grades, attendance, assignments, notices, and teacher messages. Ask for translated instructions or interpretation.
  • Check attendance and grades weekly; contact teachers early instead of waiting for report cards.
  • Keep report cards, transcripts, test scores, IEP/504 documents, awards, aid letters, and application logins in one folder.
  • Ask the school what language-access services are available for meetings and important notices.

Who can help

  • Teachers: course expectations, tutoring, recommendation letters.
  • Counselor: graduation plan, schedules, college list, referrals.
  • College/career advisor: applications, essays, aid, scholarships.
  • Multilingual and special education staff: language services and accommodations.
  • Schedule at least one family meeting each year and leave with a written action list.
Activities, career exploration, and summer opportunities

Beyond the classroom

  • Meaningful activities include clubs, sports, arts, volunteering, faith/community groups, work, caring for siblings, and family responsibilities.
  • Depth and growth matter more than joining many groups. Track roles, hours, projects, awards, and impact for future applications.
  • Explore career and technical education (CTE), job shadowing, internships, apprenticeships, and industry credentials.

Use summers wisely

  • Consider enrichment, work, volunteering, a college visit, test preparation, or a program tied to an interest.
  • Before an outside or online class, confirm cost, approval, credit value, and whether it affects graduation, college admission, or NCAA eligibility.
  • Ask about transportation, meal support, technology, fee waivers, and program deadlines.
High school roadmap: what to do each year

9th grade

  • Learn credits and portal.
  • Build attendance and study habits.
  • Join interests; start activity list.
  • Review next year’s placement.

10th grade

  • Check GPA and credit audit.
  • Try advanced, CTE, or dual-credit courses if appropriate.
  • Explore careers and options.
  • Plan a useful summer.

11th grade

  • Take sustainable rigor.
  • Make a college/training list.
  • Check SAT/ACT policies; test only if useful or required.
  • Request recommendations early.

12th grade

  • Confirm graduation status.
  • Apply by every deadline.
  • Complete FAFSA or state aid application as soon as it opens.
  • Compare offers and send final transcript.
College and training choices, applications, and admissions

Good options after high school

  • Community college: associate degrees, certificates, career programs, and transfer pathways.
  • Four-year college: bachelor’s programs at public or private schools; cost and admission differ.
  • Trade/technical school or apprenticeship: focused career training. Compare licensing, completion rates, total cost, and job outcomes.
  • Work, service, or a gap year: can be intentional with a plan; check deferred admission and aid rules.

The common application process

  1. Build a balanced list based on academics, cost, programs, location, and support.
  2. Check deadlines, essay questions, test policy, recommendations, transcripts, and fee waivers.
  3. Apply through the school’s website or Common App when available.
  4. Monitor email and college portals for missing items; save all submissions.
  5. Compare offers by net cost, grants/scholarships, work-study, and loans—not headline price alone.
Financial aid and succeeding once college starts

Paying for college

  • FAFSA is the main federal aid form; states and colleges may require additional forms. Confirm current deadlines.
  • Students not eligible for federal aid may have state alternative applications or institutional scholarships; ask a counselor or aid office.
  • Prioritize grants and scholarships before loans. Check renewal requirements such as GPA and enrollment.
  • Never pay someone to submit a free aid form; watch for scholarship scams.

A successful first year

  • Attend orientation and meet an advisor before course registration.
  • Know degree-credit requirements and make a semester plan.
  • Use tutoring, writing center, library, disability, counseling, career, and basic-needs services early.
  • For transfer, follow an official transfer agreement and save syllabi and transcripts.
  • Each term, check grades, academic standing, aid renewal, and account balance.
Family checklist for this month
  • Log into the portal and review attendance, grades, and deadlines.
  • Request the current transcript and graduation-credit audit.
  • Set one academic goal, one career/activity goal, and one question for the school.
  • Find the next family night, college fair, aid workshop, or registration deadline.
  • Ask about interpretation, tutoring, transportation, technology, and fee-waiver support.
  • Confirm important requirements directly with the high school, college, or official agency.
